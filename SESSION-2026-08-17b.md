## 2. What the module now does, and why each step exists

Four steps. Every constant traces to a measurement recorded beside it in the file.

1. **Pixels → brightness temperature.** Not brightness. GIBS renders GOES and
   Himawari as a grey ramp *plus a coloured section for tops colder than about
   −12 °C*, so brightness is **not monotonic in temperature** — measured over
   North America, the deepest tops (−90 to −50 °C) average channel value 131 while
   mid cloud (−20 to 0 °C) averages 163 and bare ground 112. Any rule written on
   brightness erases the deepest cloud.
2. **Cloud = depression below that pixel's own clear-sky temperature.**
3. **Depression → probability**, from a curve measured against EUMETSAT's mask.
4. **Satellites combined by viewing geometry**, cos³ to the limb.

Rendering: opacity is the probability; colour is the depression (cloud-top
height). Drawn only where P ≥ 0.5 — "more likely cloud than not". At P > 0.02 it
painted 96.8% of a real view; at 0.5, 66.4%, which is the observed global cloud
fraction.

## 3. Findings that cost the most to get. Do not re-derive these.

### 3.1 The temperature decode has two traps, both silent

- **Greys above 178.** The published colour map tabulates its grey ramp only to
  179; GOES routinely sends greys to 197. Nearest-colour matching those against
  the full table returned **grey 190 → +54 °C** and **grey 182 → −64 °C**. The
  coldest greys decoded as *hot*. The tabulated ramp is linear to within 0.24 °C
  over its 138 entries, so greys are decoded from that line and extrapolated:
  `T = -0.38598 * grey + 57.2375`.
- **Coloured pixels must only match coloured entries.** A desaturated blend at a
  colour boundary lands nearest a *grey* table entry, and every grey entry is
  warm — measured, coloured pixels decoding as high as **+39 °C**. That turned the
  coldest storm cores into clear sky and punched white holes through them. The
  cube is now built from entries colder than −11.5 °C only, and the grey test is
  on **saturation** (`max−min ≤ 12`), not channel differences, so antialiasing
  between two greys still reads as grey.

### 3.2 The clear-sky reference must be TEMPORAL, not spatial

Cloud moves; terrain does not. The clear-sky value of a pixel is the warmest that
pixel has been across recent frames.

Taking it from neighbouring pixels cannot distinguish cold cloud from cold ground,
so **every highland reads as cloud**. Measured against GOES's own simultaneous
visible band: Denver, at 1600 m and plainly sunny, sat 5.9 °C below the warmest
land within reach and was drawn as cloud. With a temporal background it sits 1.1 °C
below its own clear-sky value and is correctly clear.

Two further things, both learned the hard way:

- **Nothing spatial belongs on top of it.** An intermediate version kept the old
  cell percentile and neighbour search as well — warmest of warmest of warmest —
  and painted **81%** of the United States against a visible band saying 20%.
  Using the background directly: **22%**.
- **Frames must be a day apart, not hours.** At 4-hour spacing, cloud that sits
  over a place for an afternoon becomes its own clear-sky value and the storm
  punches holes in itself — visible as a moth-eaten layer. Same time of day on
  the previous four days has no diurnal bias to correct. The published method
  (bispectral composite threshold, Jedlovec et al.) uses **twenty days of the
  second-warmest value**; this is the same idea at four days. Second-warmest, not
  warmest, so one bad scan line cannot set a pixel's reference.

### 3.3 The background grid must be the whole world

Sized to the satellite (`lon ± 80°`) it wrapped past 180° for Himawari and both
GOES, and the wrap fallback then requested the world at the *same pixel width* —
so three of five satellites measured cloud against a clear-sky field four times
coarser than their own imagery. **That was the "Minecraft blocks", and the missing
slices were its off-disc gaps.** One fixed world grid at 1024 (0.35°) has no wrap
case to get wrong.

### 3.4 `map.getBounds()` is unusable in globe projection

It reports the whole world at almost any zoom. `cloud.js` guards this by falling
back to the entire globe, which is harmless for a climatology and fatal here: the
module was fetching **five satellites at world extent on every pan**. Confirmed by
`Satellite.diagnose()` reporting `drawn:{w:-180,e:180}` while the map showed
Iberia. The box now comes from zoom and centre arithmetically — MapLibre's world is
exactly `512 * 2^zoom` pixels across.

### 3.5 Request count, and where the time went

- Satellites must be chosen **per antimeridian half**, not per view. Chosen for the
  whole view, a Pacific view asked Meteosat about the far side of the world; those
  return an empty image, indistinguishable from a not-yet-published frame, so each
  burned the full step-back retry. Measured: **45 requests → 15**.
- Background frames are independent — fetch them **in parallel**, and start every
  satellite's background before any imagery. Sequentially they were four round
  trips per satellite before anything could draw.
- Measured request cost: 850 px 0.5 s, 1100 px 1.6 s. Canvas is capped at 1024;
  below about 720 across a hemisphere a pixel is 30 km and cloud can only look
  blocky whatever else is right.

### 3.6 Rendering decisions that were argued over and settled

- **Not white.** sat24's clouds read white because its background is black; on the
  near-white street and topographic basemaps white cloud is invisible.
- **Not blue.** Every basemap renders water blue and most of what this layer
  covers is ocean.
- **Red**, accepting a partial clash with the track and umbra: those are thin
  lines over a filled area, and the cloud must be legible.
- Opacity must not start at zero — a thin deck blocks totality as completely as a
  thunderstorm.
- An empty frame is **not an error**: GIBS answers a not-yet-published time with a
  valid, entirely transparent PNG. Himawari was measured returning three in a row.
  Test the PIXELS. A dropped satellite is a hole and **a hole reads as clear sky**.

## 4. What infrared cannot do

It cannot see cloud nearly as warm as the ground beneath it. Low marine stratus is
the clearest case — over the open Atlantic this under-reports against EUMETSAT's
mask — and it is exactly the cloud that ruins an eclipse.

The fix is a channel that **ADDS** cloud (the visible band by day, or `msg_fes:clm`
where published), never a constant that inflates this one. Gating on the coarse
mask was tried and produced tile edges; if it comes back it must add, not gate.

## 5. THE OPEN DEFECT

On views that **cross the antimeridian**, a blank vertical stripe about 50 canvas
columns wide appears at the boundary between the two halves. Reproduced in the
harness: `mkframes.py 175 20 2.2 f_pac.json` then `fullpreview.js`, which reports
`empty columns 49/1024` and renders the stripe visibly.

Already ruled out, with numbers:

- **Not missing imagery.** All eight frames carry data; GOES-West and GOES-East
  both report 0.79 alpha coverage over that half.
- **Not the split.** Every canvas column maps into exactly one half — traced
  column by column, no overlap and no gap.
- **Not viewing geometry.** Both satellites covering the stripe have positive
  weight there.

So it is in `bgAt()` or in the `srcX` mapping. `bgAt` returns −999 if **any** of
its four bilinear neighbours is missing, and pixels are skipped when it does —
that is the first thing to instrument. Print, for one column inside the stripe,
each frame's `srcX`, its weight, and what `bgAt` returns.

Do not guess at this. It reproduces on demand in about three minutes.

## 6. The harness — keep this, it is the point

- `tools/checks/mkframes.py LON LAT ZOOM out.json` — fetches a real
  multi-satellite composite for that view, including each satellite's four-day
  background, exactly as the module builds them.
- `tools/checks/fullpreview.js out.json out.ppm` — runs the **shipped**
  `compose()` from `js/satellite.js` on those pixels, reports drawn percentage and
  empty columns, and writes an image. Convert with PIL and **open it**.

Verified through this path: the North America composite (three satellites) renders
with zero empty columns at 34% drawn, and reads as weather — frontal bands, wisps,
fine texture.

Also worth keeping: `tools/checks/calibrate_cloud.py`, which scores the module
against EUMETSAT's operational cloud mask over held-out scenes with no human in
the loop, and `test_satellite.js`, which now fails if any of the deleted hacks
reappears (median filter, edge feather, share threshold, shade floor, spatial
ground search).

  second, plus the earlier deploys. Nearly all of them were the assistant guessing
  at appearance from prose.
