1. What is this app?

Who doesn't love an eclipse? Earth–Moon–Sun all in a straight line? Amazing. The Sun using the Moon to point at a specific place on land? Even better. So here is a tool to help you get to that spot, where you can witness the sky while, like a giant coronal iris with a razor-sharp pupil at its centre, the sky stares back at you.

In case you want to go back in time, this app covers ~11,800 solar eclipses — a five-millennium range. Pick an eclipse, click a location, get exact local circumstances — sunrise/set, contact times, sun position, cloud outlook, terrain shadows. Works fully offline once installed, which is the point: it's built for the field.

Install: on phone, browser share button → Add to Home Screen. Runs full-screen, keeps offline data, behaves like a native app.

2. Search syntax

Rich search is important. I saw an eclipse when I was a kid. When's the next eclipse on my birthday? When's the next eclipse in Hanimadhoo? Was there an eclipse on my birthday in Hanimadhoo when I was a kid?

Type any combination of these into the search box, in any order, separated by spaces:

Example: total 1900-2026 Burgos — shows both total eclipses in Burgos, 121 years apart.

Countries

A country stands for all of itself: chile total asks whether the eclipse reached anywhere in Chile. Names take several forms — usa, us, United States, United States of America, and the two- and three-letter codes.

Four things differ from a city:

  • Where a city and a country share a name the city wins — singapore means the city. Add “country” to force the other reading.
  • A country means its own land. France is France and Corsica, not French Guiana, so an eclipse crossing South America won't answer to france. The far-flung parts are searchable separately: french guiana, réunion, martinique, caribbean netherlands. Accents optional.
  • Obscuration is the best figure anywhere inside the borders, to the nearest 5% — it tells you the eclipse was worth travelling for, not where to stand.
  • Eclipses covering less than a fifth of the Sun at their best are left out entirely.

Writing place names

Multi-word names work as you'd write them: new york, ho chi minh, cape town. You don't need quotes.

paris france total works too — the country just says which Paris, and filters nothing.

Clicking the map replaces whatever place you'd typed, keeping the eclipse type.

How a place changes "type"

  • total — eclipses which are total anywhere.
  • total paris, chile total — only eclipses which appear total from that place.
  • italy total >90 — adding an obscuration widens it back to total anywhere, kept only where your place sees at least that partial percentage. In August 1999 the path passed just north of Italy, so italy total finds nothing while italy total >90 finds it.
  • Obscuration is the Sun's covered area at a selected location, or the maximum anywhere on Earth if you haven't set one.

Search range

Info → Settings → Search range caps how much of the catalogue is scanned (Modern 1500–2500 / ±500 years / Extended −1000–3000 / All ~11,800). Smaller ranges search faster. Typing an explicit year in the search box always overrides this setting for that search.

3. On the map

The globe itself, and the controls that live on top of it. On desktop it's the permanent centre view; on mobile it's the Map tab.

  • Click anywhere to set your viewing location. This updates the Details panel with circumstances for the selected eclipse at that point, and adds the coordinates to the search field.
  • 📍 button uses your device GPS to set the location.
  • Basemap picker (top right) gives you three options when online — Street, Topo, Satellite. Offline, the globe automatically falls back to a shaded-relief map with coastlines, borders, rivers and city labels.

Overlay buttons (top left)

◐ Terrain shadows — so you can see where the eclipse will actually be viewable given the local terrain.

  • Needs a live connection (streams elevation data) — greyed out offline.
  • Zoom in to activate — shadows only render above a certain zoom.
  • A time scrubber appears at the bottom: drag to watch shadows lengthen/shorten through the event. The clock follows whichever local/UT mode you've picked in Details.
  • You can also click a row in the Details contact-times table to scrub directly to that contact time.

☁ Cloud cover — three data sources, one button. It reveals:

  • Average: Long-run cloud-cover climatology for the date. Precached — works offline.
  • Now: A live cloud mask, online-only, decoded from current infrared satellite data.
  • Photo: The actual live satellite picture, online-only, for more detailed cloud data on the day.

4. Details tab

Everything the app knows about the selected eclipse, in two parts: what it looks like from your location, and the event's global facts, which don't depend on where you're standing.

Local Circumstances

Shown once you've set a location (search by coordinates, or click the map). A summary — duration, magnitude, obscuration, sun position at maximum — leads, because that's the question you opened the panel to answer.

The last row, Clear sky, is the odd one out: it says nothing about the eclipse, only about your chances of seeing it. It's the share of the time this spot is historically cloud-free at that time of day and that time of year, from the satellite record. Higher is better, like everything else in the block. It is not a forecast — for an eclipse in 2085 no such thing exists, and even for next month this is a long-run average rather than a prediction.

Contact Times

C1 (partial begins) → C2 (totality/annularity begins, umbral events only) → MAX (greatest eclipse) → C3 → C4, each with sun altitude and azimuth. Click any row to jump the terrain-shadow scrubber to that instant.

Each row carries a small icon showing the Sun–Moon overlap oriented as you'd actually see it — the angle is measured clockwise from your zenith, so the icon tells you which side of the Sun's disc the Moon is covering at that moment, not just that a contact is happening.

Click the column header to switch every time in the table (and the shadow-scrubber clock) between local time and UT. Persists across sessions. Times off the greatest-eclipse date carry a (±Nd) suffix.

Sky Tracker

A small interactive sky diagram — the Sun's path across your sky through the whole event, drawn to true scale, so distances on it are real distances in the sky. Drag its slider to scrub a Sun marker along the arc and watch the Moon's bite grow and shrink at each instant, in sync with the contact-times table above.

Where the eclipse is total at your location, the planets and the bright stars appear around the Sun between second and third contact, each where you would actually see it.

Global Circumstances

Reference facts about the eclipse itself, independent of any location — shown at the bottom since it's the least actionable part: time and place of greatest eclipse (UT), sun altitude/azimuth there, magnitude, path width, longest duration (and where), Saros number, and ΔT used for the calculation.

Save & share

Two icons sit beside the date:

  • saves the eclipse — with your currently set location, if any — to your Log.
  • the share icon builds a text summary (circumstances plus a link back to this exact eclipse and location) and hands it to your device's native share sheet, or opens a copy-to-clipboard box if the browser has none.

5. Log tab

Your personal list of saved/seen eclipses.

Stored only on this device — no account, nothing uploaded.

Per-entry controls, left to right:

IconAction
→ Mark as seen — the flag fills in once toggled on.
Overwrite the entry's saved location with the map's current pin.
Remove the eclipse from the list.
Jump to — restores this eclipse and its saved location on the map.
Checkbox — include this eclipse on the poster map (below).

Toolbar, below the list:

IconAction
Make map — see Poster maker, below.
Select all / clear selection for the poster (a dash instead of a tick means only some rows are picked).
Export log — downloads your whole log as a dated .json file. This is your entire backup story, so do it before switching devices or clearing browser data.
Import log — merges a previously exported .json file back in (adds new entries, updates matching ones).

Poster maker

Tap Make map to open a full-screen "umbral paths" map built from your Log selection — every checked eclipse's path on one world map. If nothing is checked, it uses your whole log. Options:

  • Projection: Natural Earth, Equal Earth, Sinusoidal, Plate Carrée, Robinson, Mollweide, Winkel Tripel
  • Theme: T-Shirt, Midnight, Blueprint, Mono Ink
  • Centring: Greenwich, Asia, Americas, Pacific
  • Export as SVG (vector, editable) or PNG (raster, ready to print/share)

Good for a "my eclipses" wall map or literally printing on a shirt.

6. Info tab

Instructions

Data — sources & accuracy

Eclipse data

  • Catalogue & Besselian elements: Espenak & Meeus, Five Millennium Canon of Solar Eclipses (NASA/TP-2006-214141); local circumstances via Meeus's Besselian-element method (Astronomical Algorithms, 2nd ed.).
  • ΔT (TT−UT): USNO observed monthly data (1973–Apr 2026) → USNO quarterly forecast (through late 2033) → Espenak–Meeus polynomial (−720 to 2050) → Stephenson/Morrison/Hohenkerk (2016) extrapolation outside that.
  • 94 eclipses have totality somewhere but no central line (the shadow axis misses Earth while the cone's edge still clips it) — the canon correctly records zero central duration for these; the app additionally computes a longest totality found on the surface for them.

Maps

  • Street & Sat: Esri World Street Map and World Imagery (esri.com).
  • Topo: Esri World Topographic zoomed out, handing over to OpenTopoMap (CC-BY-SA, opentopomap.org) close in.
  • Offline fallback: NASA/NGDC Natural Earth II shaded relief, with coastlines, borders, rivers, lakes and cities from Natural Earth public-domain geodata (naturalearthdata.com). Country names and their alternative spellings, used by country search, come from the same source.
  • Rendered with MapLibre GL JS (BSD-3-Clause, maplibre.org).

Weather / cloud

  • Average mode: ERA5 reanalysis total cloud cover (Copernicus/ECMWF), 1991–2020 mean.
  • Now and Photo modes: live geostationary infrared and imagery from NASA GIBS and EUMETSAT (GOES, Himawari and Meteosat).

Other

  • Timezones resolved offline via a polygon lookup.
  • Elevation auto-filled online via the Open-Elevation API.

About

My name is Guy, and I have been following eclipses since a fateful camping trip back in May 1994. We all ate mushrooms and sat in a circle with drums between our knees, silver glasses on our faces, grinning like fools and staring upwards at the ring of fire. That kind of sealed the deal, and certainly changed my life. Since then, I have always had a direction — an endless series of predictable glorious events — to pursue: across mountains, jungles, and deserts, to the very farthest corners of the globe.

Although there are many eclipse-adventure-planning tools out there (Thank you, Mr. Espenak! Merci, M. Jubier!), I still missed certain features: search-by-date (all the eclipses on your birthday), search-by-location (what, no TSEs in Ouagadougou since the year 913?!), search-by-vibe (was there a total eclipse right near but not in Oslo sometime in the 50s?), historical cloud data overlays (did he just use the C-word?), shadow-mapping from local topographies (d'oh that hill is in the way!), and offline map caching for in-the-field use. This app tries to cover all the bases in one convenient package, to help plan syzygystic adventures more efficiently. I also learned a ton about Besselian geometry while making it, which is always so much fun.

On a phone, add it to your home screen. Use your browser's share button and choose "Add to Home Screen". Installed, it runs full-screen, keeps its offline data, and behaves like an app rather than a web page — which is the point, since it is built to work in a field with no signal.

Tuned to Chrome + iOS and never tested on Android — if you have any suggestions or find any bugs, drop me a line — and don't stare at the sun!

8. Tips

  • Combine location + obscuration to plan a "partial-but-worth-it" trip near a totality path.
  • A saved log entry keeps whatever location was set when you saved it — use the pencil icon if you decide on a different viewing spot later.
  • The share text is plain, tab-formatted — pastes cleanly into notes apps or messages.
  • If cloud "Now"/"Photo" look greyed out, check you're online and not above ~65°N — geostationary satellites don't see that far north.
