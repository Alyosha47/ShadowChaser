1. Syzygy

Who doesn't love an eclipse? Earth–Moon–Sun all in a straight line? Amazing. The Sun using the Moon to point at a specific place on land? Even better. So here is a tool to help you get to that spot, where you can witness the sky while, like a giant coronal iris with a razor-sharp pupil at its centre, the sky stares back at you.

In case you want to go back in time, this app covers ~11,800 solar eclipses — a five-millennium range. Pick an eclipse, click a location, get exact local circumstances — sunrise/set, contact times, sun position, cloud outlook, terrain shadows. Works fully offline once installed, which is the point: it's built for the field.

Install: on phone, browser share button → Add to Home Screen. Runs full-screen, keeps offline data, behaves like a native app.

2. Search

Rich search is important. I saw an eclipse when I was a kid. When's the next eclipse on my birthday? When's the next eclipse in Hanimadhoo? Was there an eclipse on my birthday in Hanimadhoo when I was a kid?

Example: total 1900-2026 Burgos — shows both total eclipses in Burgos, 121 years apart.

Countries

A country stands for all of itself: chile total asks whether the eclipse reached anywhere in Chile. Names take several forms — usa, us, United States, United States of America, and the two- and three-letter codes.

Four things differ from a city:

  • Where a city and a country share a name the city wins — armenia means the city in Colombia; armenia country means the country.
  • A country means its own land. France is France and Corsica, not French Guiana, so an eclipse crossing South America won't answer to france. The far-flung parts are searchable separately: french guiana, réunion, martinique, caribbean netherlands. Accents optional.
  • Obscuration is the best figure anywhere inside the borders, to the nearest 5% — it tells you the eclipse was worth travelling for, not where to stand.
  • Eclipses covering less than a fifth of the Sun at their best are left out entirely.

Writing place names

Multi-word names work as you'd write them: new york, ho chi minh, cape town. You don't need quotes.

paris france total works too — the country just says which Paris, and filters nothing.

Clicking the map replaces whatever place you'd typed, keeping the eclipse type.

How a place changes "type"

  • total — eclipses which are total anywhere.
  • total paris, chile total — only eclipses which appear total from that place.
  • italy total >90 — adding an obscuration widens it back to total anywhere, kept only where your place sees at least that partial percentage. In August 1999 the path passed just north of Italy, so italy total finds nothing while italy total >90 finds it.
  • Obscuration is the Sun's covered area at a selected location, or the maximum anywhere on Earth if you haven't set one.

3. Map

The globe itself, and the controls that live on top of it. On desktop it's the permanent centre view; on mobile it's the Map tab.

  • Click anywhere to set your viewing location. This updates the Details panel with circumstances for the selected eclipse at that point, and adds the coordinates to the search field.
  • 📍 button uses your device GPS to set the location.
  • Basemap picker (top right) gives you three options when online — Street, Topo, Satellite. Offline, the globe automatically falls back to a shaded-relief map with coastlines, borders, rivers and city labels.

Overlay buttons (top left)

Terrain shadows — so you can see where the eclipse will actually be viewable given the local terrain.

  • Needs a live connection (streams elevation data) — greyed out offline.
  • Zoom in to activate — shadows only render above a certain zoom.
  • A time scrubber appears at the bottom: drag to watch shadows lengthen/shorten through the event. The clock follows whichever local/UT mode you've picked in Details.
  • You can also click a row in the Details contact-times table to scrub directly to that contact time.

Cloud cover — three data sources, one button. It reveals:

  • Average: Long-run cloud-cover climatology for the date. Precached — works offline.
  • Now: A live cloud mask, online-only, decoded from current infrared satellite data.
  • Photo: The actual live satellite picture, online-only, for more detailed cloud data on the day.

Favorability — the other two overlay buttons each show you one ingredient. This one blends them into a single answer to “how good is this spot for watching this eclipse”, painted along the central path only, blue for better and red for worse. Total and annular eclipses only; the button does nothing for a partial one, which has no central path.

Three criteria — duration, sun altitude and the average cloudiness — each measured at that spot’s own local maximum, and multiplied, so one bad ingredient cannot be bought off by the other two:

S = (1−C)0.60 × (T/Tmax)0.40 × (A/7.5)0.35 C cloud fraction T totality length Tmax longest totality on path A sun altitude in °, capped at 7.5°
  • The scale is relative to the path, not absolute. Colours are stretched across the range that particular eclipse offers, so blue means “the best this eclipse has” and not “good”. 2033 will almost certainly be cloudy along its whole track, and the map will still show you where the best of it is. The same blue on two different eclipses does not mean the same thing.
  • Whole path / This view. Whole path keeps that scale fixed wherever you pan. This view restretches to what is on screen, so once you have chosen a country you can still see contrast within it — the colours then change as you move, which is the point rather than a fault.
  • Zoom in and terrain joins in — ground where a ridge blocks the Sun is painted dark red. That is not a low score but a disqualification, which is why it has its own colour rather than the bottom of the ramp. It streams elevation data, so it needs a connection, and it is drawn for one instant: the local maximum at the centre of your view, which re-centres as you move.
  • What it does not know: roads, access, or local microclimate. It will quite happily recommend the middle of an ocean. It is a guide to the sky, not to the journey.
  • Precached apart from the terrain — the score itself works offline.

4. Details

Everything the app knows about the selected eclipse, in two parts: what it looks like from your location, and the event's global facts, which don't depend on where you're standing.

Local Circumstances

Shown once you've set a location (search by coordinates, or click the map). A summary — duration, magnitude, obscuration, sun position at maximum

Favorability computes a summary of the rows above rather than another measurement — the same blended score the ☺ overlay paints, for the spot you have selected. Hover it (or tap it) for the three numbers behind it. It appears only inside the central path, where the score means something.

The row above it, Clear sky, is the odd one out: it says nothing about the eclipse, only about your chances of seeing it. It's the share of the time this spot has been historically cloud-free at that time of day and that time of year, from the satellite record. Higher is better, like everything else in the block. It is not a forecast — for an eclipse in 2085 no such thing exists, and even for next month this is a long-run average rather than a prediction.

Contact Times

C1 (partial begins) → C2 (totality/annularity begins, umbral events only) → MAX (greatest eclipse) → C3 → C4, each with sun altitude and azimuth. Click any row to jump the sky-tracker (and terrain-shadow scrubber, if it's open) to that instant.

Each row carries a small icon showing the Sun–Moon overlap oriented as you'd actually see it — the angle is measured clockwise from your zenith, so the icon tells you which side of the Sun's disc the Moon is covering at that moment, not just that a contact is happening.

Click the column header to switch every time in the table (and the shadow-scrubber clock) between local time and UT. Persists across sessions.

Sky Tracker

A small interactive sky diagram — the Sun's path across your sky through the whole event, drawn to true scale, so distances on it are real distances in the sky. Drag its slider to scrub a Sun marker along the arc and watch the Moon's bite grow and shrink at each instant, in sync with the contact-times table above.

Where the eclipse is total at your location, the planets and the bright stars appear around the Sun between second and third contact, each where you would actually see it.

Global Circumstances

Reference facts about the eclipse itself, independent of any location: time and place of greatest eclipse (UT), sun altitude/azimuth there, magnitude, path width, longest duration (and where), Saros number, and ΔT used for the calculation.

Save & share

Three icons sit beside the date:

  • the globe icon downloads a KMZ for Google Earth — one eclipse per file, named for its date and type. Inside: the centreline and umbra limits, the penumbral and terminator limits, the maximum-on-the-horizon curve, the shadow's own footprint at intervals, and greatest eclipse.
  • the share icon builds a text summary (circumstances plus a link back to this exact eclipse and location) and hands it to your device's native share sheet, or opens a copy-to-clipboard box if the browser has none.
  • saves the eclipse — with your currently set location, if any — to your Log.

The diamonds along the centreline — one roughly every 100 km — are the useful part. Each one already carries its own contact times: click it in Google Earth and the balloon gives C1 to C4, duration, sun altitude and azimuth, and obscuration for that exact spot, in UT and in local time.

5. Log

Your personal list of saved/seen eclipses.

Stored only on this device — no account, nothing uploaded.

Per-entry controls, left to right:

IconAction
→ Mark as seen — the flag fills in once toggled on.
Overwrite the entry's saved location with the map's current pin.
Remove the eclipse from the list.
Jump to — restores this eclipse and its saved location on the map.
Checkbox — include this eclipse on the poster map (below).

Toolbar, below the list:

IconAction
Make map — see Poster maker, below.
Select all / clear selection for the poster (a dash instead of a tick means only some rows are picked).
Export log — downloads your whole log as a dated .json file. This is your entire backup story, so do it before switching devices or clearing browser data.
Import log — merges a previously exported .json file back in (adds new entries, updates matching ones).

Poster maker

Tap Make map to open a full-screen "umbral paths" map built from your Log selection — every checked eclipse's path on one world map. If nothing is checked, it uses your whole log. Options:

  • Projection: Natural Earth, Equal Earth, Sinusoidal, Plate Carrée, Robinson, Mollweide, Winkel Tripel
  • Theme: T-Shirt, Midnight, Blueprint, Mono Ink
  • Centring: Greenwich, Asia, Americas, Pacific
  • Export as SVG (vector, editable) or PNG (raster, ready to print/share)

Good for a "my eclipses" wall map or literally printing on a shirt.

Data — sources & accuracy

Eclipse data

  • Catalogue and Besselian elements: Fred Espenak and Jean Meeus, Five Millennium Canon of Solar Eclipses: −1999 to +3000 (NASA/TP-2006-214141), NASA Goddard Space Flight Center.
  • Local circumstances: the Besselian-element method of Jean Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms, 2nd ed. (Willmann-Bell, 1998), ch. 54.
  • ΔT (TT − UT): U.S. Naval Observatory observed monthly values (1973 – April 2026) → USNO quarterly forecast (to late 2033) → Espenak–Meeus polynomials (−720 to 2050, outside the USNO range) → Stephenson, Morrison & Hohenkerk (2016), Proc. R. Soc. A 472, with the 2021 addendum (Morrison, Stephenson, Hohenkerk & Zawilski), beyond that.
  • 94 eclipses have totality somewhere but no central line (the shadow axis misses Earth while the cone's edge still clips it) — the canon correctly records zero central duration for these; the app additionally computes a longest totality found on the surface for them.

Accuracy

Every curve is computed to within a few metres of its exact geometric definition — centreline, umbral and penumbral limits, sunrise/sunset curves and the maximum-on-horizon curve, all ~3 m or better. Between drawn points a line can bow a little further, up to ~10 m.

Eclipses before 1900 or after 2050 are subject to variability in ΔT.

No lunar limb correction is applied.

Maps

  • Street & Satellite: Esri World Street Map and World Imagery. Sources: Esri, Maxar, Earthstar Geographics, and the GIS User Community.
  • Topo: Esri World Topographic Map zoomed out. Close in, OpenTopoMap: map data © OpenStreetMap contributors, SRTM; map style © OpenTopoMap (CC-BY-SA).
  • Offline globe: Natural Earth II shaded relief with Natural Earth coastlines, borders, rivers, lakes and cities, by Tom Patterson and Nathaniel Vaughn Kelso; public domain. Made with Natural Earth (naturalearthdata.com). Country names and alternative spellings for country search come from the same source.
  • Terrain shadow: elevation from Terrain Tiles by Mapzen, hosted by AWS Open Data. 3DEP, SRTM and GMTED2010 content courtesy of the U.S. Geological Survey; ETOPO1 content courtesy of the U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. Further regional sources: tilezen/joerd attribution. Water mask: © OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL).
  • Elevation readout: Open-Elevation API, with Open-Meteo's elevation API as fallback (data CC BY 4.0).

Clouds

  • Average: ERA5 total cloud cover, 1991–2020 monthly means. Contains modified Copernicus Climate Change Service information 2026. Neither the European Commission nor ECMWF is responsible for any use that may be made of the Copernicus information or data it contains.
  • Now and Pic: NASA's Global Imagery Browse Services (GIBS), part of NASA's Earth Science Data and Information System (ESDIS). GOES imagery is from NOAA; Himawari imagery from the Japan Meteorological Agency; Meteosat (0° and Indian Ocean) © EUMETSAT 2026, via EUMETSAT's EUMView service.

About

My name is Guy, and I have been following eclipses since a fateful camping trip back in May 1994. We all ate mushrooms and sat in a circle with drums between our knees, silver glasses on our faces, grinning like fools and staring upwards at the ring of fire. That kind of sealed the deal, and certainly changed my life. Six months later, I was on a hilltop outside Potosi, Bolivia, and since then, I have always had a direction — an endless series of predictable glorious events — to pursue: across mountains, jungles, and deserts, to the very farthest corners of the globe.

Although there are many eclipse-adventure-planning tools out there (Thank you, Mr. Espenak! Merci, M. Jubier!), I still missed certain features: search-by-date (all the eclipses on your birthday), search-by-location (what, Ouagadougou went 894 years without a TSE?!), search-by-vibe (was there a total eclipse right near but not in Oslo sometime in the 50s?), historical cloud data overlays (did he just use the C-word?), shadow-mapping from local topographies (d'oh that hill is in the way!), and offline map caching for in-the-field use. This app tries to cover all the bases in one convenient package, to help plan syzygystic adventures more efficiently. I also learned a ton about Besselian geometry while making it, which is always so much fun.

On a phone, add it to your home screen. Use your browser's share button and choose "Add to Home Screen". Installed, it runs full-screen, keeps its offline data, and behaves like an app rather than a web page — which is the point, since it is built to work in a field with no signal.

Tuned to Chrome + iOS and never tested on Android — if you have any suggestions or find any bugs, drop me a line — and don't stare at the sun!
